# The Relay Manifesto

## Theses against cloud rent

### Where we stand

The web has been captured. What began as a network for sharing what people know has become a handful of walled gardens. Today's internet is five websites, owned by five companies, hosted on three clouds.

This was not an accident. The web began as common land: anyone could put up a page, and anyone could read it. In twenty years it was fenced off, the way the fields were fenced before it. Marx wrote that the history of enclosure is written "in letters of blood and fire". Ours is written in terms of service.

"The executive of the modern state is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie." Add the platforms, and the sentence holds. When they write the rules, they write them for themselves, and we are asked to be grateful.

> You make the thing.
> They keep it.
> You pay to reach it.

### Rent, not profit

Yanis Varoufakis calls it technofeudalism. The great platforms live less on what they sell than on rent: they own the land, and everyone who builds on it, posts on it, or sells on it pays a tithe in money, in attention, or in data. You bring in the harvest; the lord keeps it. Your friends are tenants too.

Leaving is meant to feel impossible. Your words, your photos, your audience, all of it stays behind when you go. That is not a bug in their design. It is the design.

### The magic we lost

There was a time when anyone could write an HTML file, add a little PHP, upload it to a server and have a living website. It was clumsy and it was ours.

Now the distance between "I can write a page" and "I can put something real on the web" is a chasm, filled with frameworks, build tools, pipelines and databases that take a team of specialists and a company budget. Millions of people, communities and independent voices have been pushed to the side, and told to build on platforms they do not control.

### What was taken from us

Bernard Stiegler spoke of proletarianisation: the loss of a know-how that once belonged to the people who worked. Publishing was such a know-how. A printing press, a duplicator, a radio transmitter, then a home page. It was handed over to experts and companies, and now it is rented back to us as a "service".

Relay gives it back. It is a tool for independence, built to break the grip that a few corporations hold on the web, and made for people, not for venture-funded start-ups and multinationals. Not a platform, but one program that puts what you make on the web, on a server kept by people you know. No account. No feed. No one in the middle deciding what is seen.

### The right to the web

Henri Lefebvre wrote that the right to the city "cannot be conceived of as a simple visiting right". The same is true of the web. The right to the web is not the right to scroll through what others built. It is the right to make a place there, to shape it, to change it, and to keep it.

A site on Relay is a dwelling, not a tenancy. The address is yours. The files are yours. You can pack them and move to another server whenever you like, and nobody can hold them hostage.

### Convivial tools

Ivan Illich wrote that people need "not only to obtain things", but "above all the freedom to make things among which they can live". A convivial tool is one anyone can pick up and use without asking an expert or a company for permission.

That is the whole test for Relay. If publishing needs a developer, an account, or a monthly bill, we have failed. Today it is a link someone sends you, and your work dropped onto the page.

### Against the spectacle

Guy Debord opened *The Society of the Spectacle* with this: "all of life presents itself as an immense accumulation of spectacles." The feed is that sentence made into software. It turns everything you do into something for others to watch, and turns watching into a job you do for free.

A website of your own is the opposite. It does not rank you, measure you, or sell you. It is something you make, for the people around you: the football club, the bakery, the village archive, the band, the union, your grandmother's recipes.

### Presses that cannot be bought

Walter Benjamin asked writers not only to fill the apparatus of publishing, but to change it. A Relay server is a small piece of that apparatus, in the hands of the people who use it. A collective keeps one running for its members. A neighbourhood keeps one for its street. Each one is a printing press that no investor can buy and no shareholder can shut down.

There is no centre to capture. Every server runs the same free software. If one closes, the sites move to another. One collective's server is a club. A thousand, side by side, are the commons.

### What we promise

**You hold the key.** An address belongs to whoever holds its key, not to whoever runs the server.

**No accounts, ever.** There is no Relay account and there never will be. Nobody keeps a list of everyone.

**Your work leaves with you.** Everything can be packed and carried to another server. Nothing is kept hostage.

**No tracking, no feed.** No algorithm decides who sees your site. No one follows your visitors.

**Small on purpose.** Every feature is a tax on the people who did not ask for it. We add one only when it earns its place.

**Owned by no one.** Free software, kept alive by the people who run it. No venture capital, no growth plan, no exit.

### Where we are going

What works today is publishing: you drop what you made, and it is online. The next version, Punk, goes further. None of this is built yet; it is where we are heading.

**No server needed.** Every visitor helps carry the page. Opening a site is enough to help keep it online.

**Every page can be edited and copied.** An editor in the browser, so anyone can change a page or start their own from someone else's.

**Simple or as complex as you like.** Markdown for a page of text, small scripts for something that moves. You open a site and start, with nothing to install.

### Take it back

None of this is destiny. The machines that fenced the web can unfence it, if enough people keep a server running and enough people put their work there instead of in someone else's field.

They took the land from under our feet. They are taking the ground under our words. Take it back.
